Fluid Mechanics: Mathematical Preliminaries

Introduction

We will learn about the physics of fluid flow. However, we will need a unified language to describe this physics. As is common in engineering, we will rely on mathematics as the language for describing fluid flows. Specifically, vectors and vector calculus will comprise the building blocks.

Scalars and vectors are like the ’alphabets’ of the physics descriptions. Scalars are quantities that are described using only a Numeric Value describing the how much of the quantity is being represented? or the Magnitude of the quantity. For example: pressure, temperature etc. Vectors are quantities that are described using a numeric value and a direction, describing the Magnitude of the quantity, along a specific direction. For example: velocity, force etc. We will also refer to a set of quantities called Tensors, which we will refer to as quantities of higher-order than scalars, and vectors - and in plain terms, we will mean that they are described using more than one index/component-count system (as opposed to 0 indices for scalars, and 1 index for vectors). The common way to think about Tensors would be, tensors of second-order are equivalent to matrices, with 2 indices, where the indices indicate row and column positioning.

Notation

Throughout our description, we will write vectors with 1 underline. For example: \(\underbar{$\bm{x}$}\), \(\underbar{$\bm{a}$}\), \(\underbar{$\bm{n}$}\), etc. Keeping with the notion that an underline indicates the index-count/order of the mathematical ’alphabet’ of ours - scalars will have No underline, and matrices will have 2 underlines. Also, vectors which have a net magnitude of 1 are called unit vectors (represented using a hat or \(\wedge\)). For example: \(\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}\), \(\hat{\underbar{$\bm{a}$}}\), \(\hat{\underbar{$\bm{n}$}}\), etc.

Equivalent Notations

Commonly, we will write a vector in terms of unit vectors and components along the basis coordinate axes (\(x,y,z\) axes; \(1,2,3\) axes):

\[\begin{align} \underbar{$\bm{u}$} &= u_1\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{1} + u_2\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{2} + u_3\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{3} \\[4pt] \underbar{$\bm{u}$} &= u\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{1} + v\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{2} + w\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{3} \\[4pt] \underbar{$\bm{u}$} &= u\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}+ v\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{y}$}}+ w\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{z}$}}\\[4pt] \underbar{$\bm{u}$} &= u_1\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{e}$}}_{1} + u_2\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{e}$}}_{2} + u_3\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{e}$}}_{3} \end{align}\]

(All of these notations are equivalent!)

Vector Operations

With the ’alphabets’ established, we now stitch them together to write meaningful mathematical quantities and descriptions. These rely on basic rules or operations for these objects to interplay with each other. These are the standard vector algebra operations taught in calculus classes, we simply review some of the basic ones here to get us up to speed.

Addition / Subtraction

Given two vectors:

\[\begin{align} \underbar{$\bm{u}$} &= u_1\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{1} + u_2\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{2} + u_3\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{3} \\ \underbar{$\bm{v}$} &= v_1\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{1} + v_2\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{2} + v_3\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{3} \end{align}\]

Addition (as well as subtraction) of these two can be written as:

\[\begin{equation} \underbar{$\bm{u}$}+\underbar{$\bm{v}$}= (u_1+v_1) \hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{1} + (u_2+v_2) \hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{2} + (u_3+v_3) \hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{3} \end{equation}\]

Dot Product

Given two vectors:

\[\begin{align} \underbar{$\bm{u}$} &= u_1\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{1} + u_2\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{2} + u_3\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{3} \\ \underbar{$\bm{v}$} &= v_1\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{1} + v_2\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{2} + v_3\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{3} \end{align}\]

their Dot Product, which is a scalar valued product, is stated as:

\[\begin{equation} \underbar{$\bm{u}$}\cdot\underbar{$\bm{v}$} = u_1 v_1 + u_2 v_2 + u_3 v_3 \end{equation}\]

And:

\[\begin{equation} \sqrt{\underbar{$\bm{u}$}\cdot\underbar{$\bm{u}$}} = \sqrt{u_1^2 + u_2^2 + u_3^2} \end{equation}\]

gives us the magnitude of \(\underbar{$\bm{u}$}\), or \(\|\underbar{$\bm{u}$}\|\).

Interpretation of dot product:

\(\underbar{$\bm{u}$}\cdot\underbar{$\bm{v}$}\) represents the component of \(\underbar{$\bm{u}$}\) projected along \(\underbar{$\bm{v}$}\) (in physics terms, think about the amount of force acting along a specific direction).

\[\begin{equation} \underbar{$\bm{u}$}\cdot\underbar{$\bm{v}$} = \|\underbar{$\bm{u}$}\|\,\|\underbar{$\bm{v}$}\|\cos(\theta) \end{equation}\]

Cross Product

Given two vectors:

\[\begin{align} \underbar{$\bm{u}$} &= u_1\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{1} + u_2\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{2} + u_3\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{3} \\ \underbar{$\bm{v}$} &= v_1\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{1} + v_2\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{2} + v_3\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{3} \end{align}\]

their Cross Product, which is a vector valued product, is stated as:

\[\begin{align} \underbar{$\bm{u}$}\times\underbar{$\bm{v}$} &= (u_2 v_3 - u_3 v_2)\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{1} \nonumber \\ &+ (u_3 v_1 - u_1 v_3)\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{2} \nonumber \\ &+ (u_1 v_2 - u_2 v_1)\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{3} \end{align}\]

or using the determinant notation:

\[\begin{equation} \underbar{$\bm{u}$}\times\underbar{$\bm{v}$} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{1} & \hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{2} & \hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{3} \\ u_1 & u_2 & u_3 \\ v_1 & v_2 & v_3 \end{vmatrix} \end{equation}\]

Interpretation of cross product:

The unit vector of the cross-product is normal to both vectors in a right-hand rule sense, with its magnitude being equal to area of parallelogram formed by the two vectors.

\[\begin{equation} \underbar{$\bm{u}$}\times\underbar{$\bm{v}$} = \|\underbar{$\bm{u}$}\|\,\|\underbar{$\bm{v}$}\|\sin(\theta)\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{n}$}} \end{equation}\]

Examples

Given a vector \(\underbar{$\bm{a}$} = 5\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{1} + 5\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{2}\), find the magnitude of \(\underbar{$\bm{a}$}\)  (\(\|\underbar{$\bm{a}$}\|\)).

Solution: \[\begin{align} \|\underbar{$\bm{a}$}\| &= \sqrt{\underbar{$\bm{a}$}\cdot\underbar{$\bm{a}$}} \\[4pt] \underbar{$\bm{a}$}\cdot\underbar{$\bm{a}$} &= \underbrace{(5\cdot5)}_{a_1^2} + \underbrace{(5\cdot5)}_{a_2^2} + \underbrace{(0\cdot0)}_{a_3^2} = 50 \end{align}\]

Hence, \(\|\underbar{$\bm{a}$}\| = \sqrt{50} = 5\sqrt{2}\).

With \(\underbar{$\bm{b}$} = 5\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{2} + 5\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{3}\) and \(\underbar{$\bm{a}$}\) given as above in Example 1, find \(\underbar{$\bm{a}$}\times\underbar{$\bm{b}$}\).

Solution: We will directly use the definition: \[\begin{align} \underbar{$\bm{a}$}\times\underbar{$\bm{b}$} &= (a_2 b_3 - a_3 b_2)\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{1} + (a_3 b_1 - a_1 b_3)\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{2} + (a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1)\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{3} \\[4pt] &= (5\cdot5 - 0\cdot5)\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{1} + (0\cdot0 - 5\cdot5)\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{2} + (5\cdot5 - 5\cdot0)\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{3} \\[4pt] &= 25\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{1} - 25\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{2} + 25\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{3} \end{align}\]

The above gives us: \[\begin{equation} \underbar{$\bm{a}$}\times\underbar{$\bm{b}$} = 25\,(\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{1} - \hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{2} + \hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{3}) \end{equation}\]

Review of Vector Calculus

Here we review common vector calculus terms that we will use: gradient, divergence, curl, laplacian. Keeping with the analogy of language to describe physics, we will position each of these quantities from the lens of their Physical meaning or interpretation. Specifically, we will discuss what each of these operations reflect about the underlying physics of flows that we want to describe.

Gradient

Assume \(\phi(\underbar{$\bm{x}$})\) is a scalar function describing a fluid flow variable (recall, for example, pressure, temperature etc.). Then the gradient of \(\phi\) is stated as:

\[\begin{equation} \nabla\phi = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_1}\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{1} + \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_2}\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{2} + \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_3}\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{3} \end{equation}\]

or

\[\begin{equation} \nabla\phi = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}} + \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{y}$}} + \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{z}$}} \end{equation}\]

Interpreting gradient as a descriptor for physics variables: Broadly, gradients relate to rates of change of physical quantities in a fluid flow. For a physical quantity \(\phi\), \(\nabla\phi\) will be perpendicular to the curves of constant \(\phi\). And: Along the direction given by \(\hat{\underbar{$\bm{n}$}}\), the rate of change is \(\nabla\phi\cdot\hat{\underbar{$\bm{n}$}}\). (Think of this as a spatial rate of change)

Divergence

Assume \(\underbar{$\bm{u}$}\) is a vector function describing a fluid flow variable (recall, for example, velocity, force etc.). Then the divergence of \(\underbar{$\bm{u}$}\) is stated as:

\[\begin{equation} \underbar{$\bm{u}$} = \underbar{$\bm{u}$}(\underbar{$\bm{x}$}) = u_1(\underbar{$\bm{x}$})\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{1} + u_2(\underbar{$\bm{x}$})\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{2} + u_3(\underbar{$\bm{x}$})\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{3} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} \nabla\cdot\underbar{$\bm{u}$} = \frac{\partial u_1}{\partial x_1} + \frac{\partial u_2}{\partial x_2} + \frac{\partial u_3}{\partial x_3} \end{equation}\]

or

\[\begin{equation} \nabla \cdot \underbar{$\bm{u}$} = \frac{\partial u_1}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial u_2}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial u_3}{\partial z} \end{equation}\]

Interpreting divergence as a descriptor for physics variables: As shown above, divergence is physically related to a measure of “stuff” going in and out of a region. This is commonly referred to as Flux. As shown in the sketch above, if net flux along the positive outward normal direction is positive (going out greater than going in), then net divergence is positive.

\[\begin{align} \Omega &\;\to\; \text{closed region (circle)} \notag \\ \hat{\underbar{$\bm{n}$}}&\;\to\; \text{outward normal on circle boundary} \notag \\ S &\;\to\; \text{boundary (perimeter of circle)} \notag \end{align}\]

There is a formal mathematical relation between flux and divergence, stated using the Divergence Theorem.

\[\begin{equation} \underbrace{\iiint_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot\underbar{$\bm{u}$}\;d\Omega}_{\displaystyle\text{Divergence}} = \underbrace{\iint_{S}\underbar{$\bm{u}$}\cdot\hat{\underbar{$\bm{n}$}}\;dS}_{\displaystyle\text{Flux}} \end{equation}\]

Curl

Assume \(\underbar{$\bm{u}$}\) is a vector function describing a fluid flow variable (recall, for example, velocity, force etc.). Then the curl of \(\underbar{$\bm{u}$}\) is stated as:

\[\begin{equation} \underbar{$\bm{u}$} = \underbar{$\bm{u}$}(\underbar{$\bm{x}$}) = u_1(\underbar{$\bm{x}$})\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{1} + u_2(\underbar{$\bm{x}$})\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{2} + u_3(\underbar{$\bm{x}$})\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{3} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{align} \nabla\times\underbar{$\bm{u}$} &= \left(\frac{\partial u_3}{\partial x_2} - \frac{\partial u_2}{\partial x_3}\right)\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{1} \\ &+ \left(\frac{\partial u_1}{\partial x_3} - \frac{\partial u_3}{\partial x_1}\right)\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{2} \\ &+ \left(\frac{\partial u_2}{\partial x_1} - \frac{\partial u_1}{\partial x_2}\right)\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{3} \end{align}\]

Interpreting curl as a descriptor for physics variables: The curl of a vector function represents the rotational parts of the vector function. This is best imagined if we truly consider \(\underbar{$\bm{u}$}\) describing the fluid flow velocity, and the flow has a swirling or rotational component in it, the curl of the velocity (as we will call it later, vorticity) is non-zero.

Laplacian

Assume \(\phi(\underbar{$\bm{x}$})\) is a scalar function describing a fluid flow variable. Then the Laplacian of \(\phi\) is stated as:

\[\begin{equation} \nabla^2\phi = \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x_1^2} + \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x_2^2} + \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x_3^2} \end{equation}\]

For vector functions \(\to\) the above definition applies component-wise for each vector component:

\[\begin{equation} \underbar{$\bm{u}$} = u_1(\underbar{$\bm{x}$})\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{1} + u_2(\underbar{$\bm{x}$})\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{2} + u_3(\underbar{$\bm{x}$})\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{3} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{align} \nabla^2\underbar{$\bm{u}$} &= \left(\frac{\partial^2 u_1}{\partial x_1^2} + \frac{\partial^2 u_1}{\partial x_2^2} + \frac{\partial^2 u_1}{\partial x_3^2}\right)\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{1} \\[6pt] &+ \left(\frac{\partial^2 u_2}{\partial x_1^2} + \frac{\partial^2 u_2}{\partial x_2^2} + \frac{\partial^2 u_2}{\partial x_3^2}\right)\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{2} \\[6pt] &+ \left(\frac{\partial^2 u_3}{\partial x_1^2} + \frac{\partial^2 u_3}{\partial x_2^2} + \frac{\partial^2 u_3}{\partial x_3^2}\right)\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{3} \end{align}\]

Interpreting Laplacian as a descriptor for physics variables: This one can be a bit tricky, but quite broadly, we encounter Laplacians a lot when we discuss physical processes that tend to smear out information in space in a uniform manner. Think some physics, in a fluid flow, that is smearing out concentration (that creamer mixing in your morning coffee without you stirring it!). We commonly call this Diffusion.

Note: Other related mathematical formulas and relations will be introduced throughout the semester as needed.

To summarize then, we have the following basic inter-relationships between physical descriptions and vector calculus operations:

  • Gradient: relates to spatial rate of change.

  • Divergence: relates to flux entering and exiting a region.

  • Curl: relates to extent of rotation.

  • Laplacian: relates to descrition of diffusion processes.

Examples

The pressure of air inside a rectangular chamber can be described as: \[\begin{equation} P(\underbar{$\bm{x}$}) = P(x,y) = \frac{x^2}{25} + \frac{y^2}{16} \end{equation}\] Find the gradient of the pressure.

Solution: Using the definition of gradient: \[\begin{equation} \nabla P = \frac{\partial P}{\partial x}\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{1} + \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{2} + \frac{\partial P}{\partial z}\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{3} \end{equation}\] \(\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial x}\) = derivative of \(P\) taken only with respect to \(x\), keeping \(y,z\) constant/fixed: \[\begin{equation} \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left[\frac{x^2}{25} + \frac{y^2}{16}\right] = \frac{2x}{25} \end{equation}\] Proceeding similarly: \[\begin{align} \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\!\left[\frac{x^2}{25}+\frac{y^2}{16}\right] = \frac{2y}{16} = \frac{y}{8} \\[6pt] \frac{\partial P}{\partial z} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\!\left[\frac{x^2}{25}+\frac{y^2}{16}\right] = 0 \end{align}\]

Putting all the pieces together: \[\begin{equation} \nabla P = \frac{2x}{25}\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{1} + \frac{y}{8}\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{2} + 0\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{3} \qquad\text{or}\qquad \nabla P = \frac{2x}{25}\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{1} + \frac{y}{8}\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}_{2} \end{equation}\]

Consider a flow velocity vector that is written as a function: \[\begin{equation} \underbar{$\bm{u}$} = (\alpha x^2 - \theta x)\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}+ (\beta y^2 + \theta y)\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{y}$}} \end{equation}\] where \(\alpha,\,\theta,\,\beta\) are constants. Calculate the divergence of this velocity vector function.

Solution: The velocity vector is: \[\begin{align} \underbar{$\bm{u}$} &= u\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}+ v\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{y}$}}+ w\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{z}$}}\\ &= (\alpha x^2-\theta x)\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}} + (\beta y^2+\theta y)\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{y}$}} + (0)\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{z}$}} \end{align}\]

Using the definition of divergence: \[\begin{equation} \nabla\cdot\underbar{$\bm{u}$} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial w}{\partial z} \end{equation}\] \(\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}\) = derivative of \(u\) taken only with respect to \(x\), keeping \(y\) and \(z\) constant/fixed: \[\begin{equation} \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\bigl[\alpha x^2 - \theta x\bigr] = 2\alpha x - \theta \end{equation}\] Proceeding similarly: \[\begin{align} \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\bigl[\beta y^2 + \theta y\bigr] = 2\beta y + \theta \\[4pt] \frac{\partial w}{\partial z} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial z}[0] = 0 \end{align}\]

Hence: \[\begin{align} \nabla\cdot\underbar{$\bm{u}$} &= (2\alpha x - \theta) + (2\beta y + \theta) + 0 \\ &= 2\alpha x + 2\beta y \end{align}\]

For the same velocity function provided in the previous example, calculate the vorticity \(\underbar{$\bm{\omega}$} = \nabla\times\underbar{$\bm{u}$}\) [the curl of the velocity]: \[\begin{equation} \underbar{$\bm{u}$} = (\alpha x^2 - \theta x)\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}+ (\beta y^2 + \theta y)\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{y}$}} \end{equation}\] Solution: We will directly use the definition of the curl: \[\begin{equation} \underbar{$\bm{u}$} = u\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}+ v\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{y}$}}+ w\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{z}$}} \end{equation}\] \[\begin{align} \nabla\times\underbar{$\bm{u}$} &= \left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial v}{\partial z}\right)\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}} + \left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\right)\hat{\underbar{$\bm{y}$}} + \left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right)\hat{\underbar{$\bm{z}$}} \end{align}\]

With \(u = \alpha x^2 - \theta x\): \(\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y} = \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial z} = 0\)

With \(v = \beta y^2 + \theta y\): \(\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial x} = 0 = \dfrac{\partial v}{\partial z}\)

Similarly, \(w = 0\): \(\dfrac{\partial w}{\partial x} = \dfrac{\partial w}{\partial y} = 0\)

Hence, plugging everything in, we get: \[\begin{equation} \underbar{$\bm{\omega}$} = \nabla\times\underbar{$\bm{u}$} = 0\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{x}$}}+ 0\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{y}$}}+ 0\,\hat{\underbar{$\bm{z}$}}= \underbar{$\bm{0}$} \end{equation}\]

Units and Dimensions

In the above descriptions, we noted the vector calculus operations on arbitrary variables (such as \(\phi\), \(\underbar{$\bm{u}$}\)) etc. In fluid mechanics, these variables can broadly be considered to describe:

  • Fluid Properties: quantities that describe some attributes of the fluid itself e.g. viscosity, density etc.

  • Flow Variables: quantities that describe attributes of the fluid flow/motion e.g. velocity, pressure etc.

Any physical quantity, scalar or vector, fluid property or flow variable, will be presented in a system of standard units. Their value will change based on the unit system of choice. Without the specified units, the numeric value associated with the physical quantity is incomplete for any real engineering work.

For example. The length of a ruler can be expressed as \[\begin{equation} 12\text{ in} \;=\; 1\text{ ft} \;=\; 30.5\text{ cm}. \end{equation}\] But it is the same amount of length expressed in different units. The underlying physical quantity that is actually being measured or quantified is called a dimension. The dimension is where we make the distinction that the length of the ruler cannot be stated in kilograms for instance.

Fundamental and Derived Dimensions

Dimensions can be of two types:

Fundamental Dimensions \[\begin{align} \text{mass} &\;\to\; M \\ \text{length} &\;\to\; L \\ \text{time} &\;\to\; T \end{align}\]

Derived Dimensions \[\begin{align} \text{speed: } \; \left[\,u\,\right] &= \frac{L}{T} \\ \text{density: } \; \left[\,\rho\,\right] &= \frac{M}{L^3} \end{align}\]

Typically dimensions are indicated using bracket notation \(\left[\,\cdot\,\right]\). In the example above, we have used the MLT system (Mass, Length, Time) of representation. There is also the FLT system (Force, Length, Time) system of representing dimension as well.

Physical quantities must be represented using the correct dimensions and using a consistent system of units.

For example.

While units of acceleration can be \(\text{m/s}^2\), \(\text{ft/s}^2\), \(\text{in/s}^2\), or \(\text{cm/s}^2\), the dimension of acceleration (in the MLT system) will always be \[\begin{equation} \left[\,\text{Acceleration}\,\right] = LT^{-2}. \end{equation}\]

Deriving Dimensions from Fundamental Equations

Say we want to obtain the dimensions of force using the fundamental dimension system \(M, L, T\).

Rule: Dimensions must match on both sides of the equation, so: \[\begin{align} \left[\,F\,\right] &= \left[\,m\,\right]\left[\,a\,\right] \\ &= M \times LT^{-2} \\ &= MLT^{-2} \quad \text{or} \quad \frac{ML}{T^2} \end{align}\]

It is more common to write the first of the two formats: e.g. \(MLT^{-2}\)

Example: Dimensions of Energy Use the same concept to find the dimensions of energy.

Solution. Recall that the fundamental relation defining energy (\(E\)) is \[\begin{align} E &= \text{Force} \times \text{Displacement} \\ &= \text{Mass} \times \text{Acceleration} \times \text{Displacement} \end{align}\] \[\begin{align} \left[\,E\,\right] &= \left[\,\text{Mass}\,\right] \cdot \left[\,\text{Acceleration}\,\right] \cdot \left[\,\text{Displacement}\,\right] \\ &= M \cdot LT^{-2} \cdot L \end{align}\] Here, \[\begin{equation} \left[\,E\,\right] = ML^2T^{-2}. \end{equation}\]

Always check the following for a given problem/solution:

  1. Are dimensions matching throughout your calculation?

  2. Do your units make sense?

  3. Are you using a consistent unit system (SI, cgs, etc.)?

  4. You cannot add/subtract quantities in different units or having different dimensions.

Do all relevant quantities or numbers have a dimension? All physical quantities do. However, we will see later on that there can be non-dimensional numbers that are important for certain types of fluid mechanics analyses. Additionally, exponents in physical equations are completely dimensionless. Meaning if we have a quantity: \(\exp{(\alpha t^2)}\) then \(\alpha t^2\) must be completely dimensionless. In both of these instances, the dimensionless quantities must have 0 exponent for each fundamental dimension: i.e.

\[\begin{equation} M^0 L^0 T^0 \rightarrow \text{dimensionless} \end{equation}\]

Dimensions of Derivatives and Integrals

How do dimensions work for derivatives? If the dimension of a quantity \(\varphi\) is \(\left[\,\varphi\,\right]\), then \[\begin{align} \left[\,\dfrac{\partial \varphi}{\partial x}\,\right] &\to \frac{\left[\,\varphi\,\right]}{L} \\ \left[\,\dfrac{\partial \varphi}{\partial t}\,\right] &\to \frac{\left[\,\varphi\,\right]}{T} \quad \text{etc.} \end{align}\]

How do dimensions work for integrals? Similarly, for a quantity \(\varphi\) with dimension \(\left[\,\varphi\,\right]\), \[\begin{align} \left[\,\int \varphi \, dx\,\right] &= \left[\,\varphi\,\right] \cdot L \\ \left[\,\int \varphi \, dt\,\right] &= \left[\,\varphi\,\right] \cdot T \end{align}\]

Example: Dimensions of Dynamic Viscosity Use the following relation, commonly called Newton’s Law of Viscosity, to find the dimensions of dynamic viscosity \(\mu\) in M.L.T system, assuming \(\tau\) is stress (force/area), and \(u\) is velocity.

\[\begin{equation} \tau = \mu \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y} \end{equation}\]

Using dimension of equation being equal on both sides:

\[\begin{equation} \left[\,\tau\,\right] = \left[\,\mu\,\right] \left[\,\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y}\,\right] \end{equation}\]

Using the rule above for dimensions of derivatives:

\[\begin{equation} \left[\,\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y}\,\right] = \frac{\left[\,u\,\right]}{L} \end{equation}\]

The dimension of stress is:

\[\begin{equation} \left[\,\tau\,\right] = \frac{\left[\,\text{Force}\,\right]}{\left[\,\text{Area}\,\right]} = \frac{MLT^{-2}}{L^2} = ML^{-1}T^{-2} \end{equation}\]

Assembling everything:

\[\begin{align} \left[\,\tau\,\right] &= \left[\,\mu\,\right] \left[\,\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y}\,\right] \\ ML^{-1}T^{-2} &= \left[\,\mu\,\right] \frac{L T^{-1}}{L} \\ \left[\,\mu\,\right] &= ML^{-1}T^{-1} \end{align}\]

Fluid Mechanics: Fundamental Properties of Fluids

What is a fluid?

We start our discussion with the fundamental question: What is a fluid? This seems, at the outset, quite intuitive. We would define a fluid as anything that flows. However, from the standpoint of precise physical description, we need to further clarify or define what Flow actually is.

A Thought Experiment:

Imagine taking a 1 cm3 block of wood, and a 1 cm3 blob of, say, honey. Imagine you place both on a rigid wooden table top.

  • What do you think will happen if you let both simply rest on the table top?

  • What do you think will happen if you start pressing and moving both around with a spoon or a stirring rod?

If you think about it, one (the blob of honey, which we will call a fluid in this experiment) will Deform while the other will not, at least not to the naked eye (the block of wood, which we call a solid instead). They both will continue to still be on the table, and even if we moved the blobs a little to the left or to the right (executing what we may call a rigid body motion), our expected behaviors in this thought experiment will not change.

Thus, Flow is intrinsically related to Deformation and does not just indicate Motion.

A fluid will deform continuously under the action of an applied (shear) force. The fluid will not return to any of its previous shapes when this force is withdrawn. That is, it takes the shape of the container. A solid will deform under the action of an applied (shear) force until its internal forces balance the applied force. The solid may retain that preferred shape, or get back to its original shape when this force is withdrawn.

The Continuum Hypothesis

The above discussion connects the fundamental definition of fluids to Flow which entails Deformation under the action of Forces. The net forces that lead to the deformations during in a fluid flow originate from applied external forces and their interplay with internal or molecular forces. In fact, the specific nature of the molecular interactions govern how the overall fluid behavior is going to be. As the individual molecules move and interact with each other at the microscale we get to see the emergent flow and deformation behavior at the macroscale.

However, for any practical fluid flow application, we cannot study the motion of each individual molecule to conduct a real-world analysis, because there are so many of them! millions, and millions, and more!. Therefore, assuming that the molecular density is high enough and the size/volume of the fluid sample is large enough, we can describe the fluid flow and behavior using Averaged or Aggregated variables and quantities. This idea is foundational to engineering analysis of fluid flows, and is referred to as the Continuum Hypothesis.

These averaged or aggregated quantities are mathematically described using variables that can change across space (\(\underbar{$\bm{x}$}\)) and time (\(t\)). As noted previously, the aggregate variables or quantities could be:

  • Variables describing Flow: common examples \(\underbar{$\bm{u}$} \left( \underbar{$\bm{x}$}, t \right)\), \(p \left( \underbar{$\bm{x}$}, t \right)\).

  • Variables describing the Fluid: common examples \(\rho \left( \underbar{$\bm{x}$}, t \right)\), \(\mu \left( \underbar{$\bm{x}$}, t \right)\).

Commonly, for such space-time varying functions, we use the term Field Variables. For example, we will commonly use descriptions of:

  • Velocity field: \(\underbar{$\bm{x}$} \left( \underbar{$\bm{x}$}, t \right)\).

  • Temperature field: \(T \left( \underbar{$\bm{x}$}, t \right)\).

  • Pressure field: \(p \left( \underbar{$\bm{x}$}, t \right)\).

Forces in a fluid

Let us now talk about the forces in a fluid,. Once averaged or aggregated (as per the continuum hypothesis) across all applied and intermolecular interaction forces for a representative Parcel of fluid, the Net/Total forces on this fluid parcel can be categorized as:

  • Body Forces: forces acting on the bulk of the fluid parcel. For example, weight, buoyancy etc.

  • Surface Forces: forces acting along the surfaces of the fluid parcel.

The surface forces, as shown in the figure above, are further categorized into Normal surface forces which act perpendicular to each surface, and Tangential surface forces acting parallel to the surface. The normal forces have the combined effect of compressing or expanding the fluid parcel, and results in what we refer to as Pressure. The tangential forces on the other hand try to slide the parallel planes of the fluid in a motion that is commonly called Shear, and consequently these tangential forces are commonly called Shear forces.
